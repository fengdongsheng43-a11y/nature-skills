# Mechanism and rival reasoning

## Purpose

Prevent a plausible story from being mistaken for the only explanation.

## Mechanism decomposition

For a proposed mechanism, write the chain as explicit transitions:

`Condition → intermediate process → measurable state change → outcome`.

Every transition should be one of:

- directly measured;
- supported by close literature;
- inferred from established principles;
- currently speculative.

Do not hide speculative transitions inside fluent prose.

## Rival classes

Generate rivals from genuinely different explanatory classes when plausible:

1. alternative mechanism at the same scale;
2. process at another scale;
3. thermodynamic equilibrium or speciation effect;
4. kinetic limitation;
5. transport or diffusion limitation;
6. interface or morphology change;
7. biological regulation or community replacement;
8. measurement or preprocessing artifact;
9. confounding or batch effect;
10. hidden compositional change;
11. reverse causation or selection effect;
12. stochastic variation.

Do not force all classes into every problem. Use only serious rivals.

## Physical–chemical–biological order of attack

When the system spans multiple domains, check the cheapest constraints first.

### Conservation and mass balance

Ask whether matter, charge, elemental inventory, energy, or electron equivalents reconcile. A mechanism that violates conservation is rejected before expensive characterization.

### Thermodynamics and speciation

Ask which phases, protonation states, complexes, redox states, or adsorption states are possible under the actual pH, ionic strength, temperature, and composition.

### Kinetics

Ask whether the proposed process can occur on the observed timescale and whether activation barriers or rate-limiting steps are plausible.

### Transport

Ask whether diffusion, convection, pore accessibility, boundary layers, or mass transfer could create the observed pattern without the proposed chemistry.

### Interfaces and structure

Ask whether surface area, defects, coordination environment, morphology, or phase boundaries change the local reaction landscape.

### Biology

Ask whether growth, adaptation, regulation, selection, turnover, or community composition can generate the same response.

### Measurement artifacts

Ask whether sampling, storage, extraction, calibration, normalization, detection limits, or data processing can create or amplify the pattern.

## Discriminating prediction

A useful prediction is not merely “the treatment improves the outcome.” It states a pattern that differs between at least two named hypotheses.

For each pair, write:

| Test | H1 predicts | H2 predicts | Indeterminate result |
|---|---|---|---|
| [measurement/intervention] | [pattern] | [different pattern] | [what would remain unresolved] |

## Death condition

A death condition is a result that materially removes the need for the claimed mechanism or contribution.

Examples of death-condition logic:

- a simpler baseline reproduces the same effect;
- the proposed mediator does not change while the outcome does;
- a mechanism-specific perturbation leaves the response unchanged;
- a measurement with sufficient resolution shows the claimed state is absent;
- the effect disappears after correcting a confounder or batch artifact.

Do not define a death condition so narrowly that the idea can never fail.
