# Evidence architecture and experiment priority

## Purpose

Turn a surviving scientific claim into the smallest coherent set of experiments that can support, weaken, or reject it.

## Claim-to-evidence chain

Use:

`Challenge → Scientific motivation → Mechanism → Prediction → Experiment → Measurement → Statistical test → Decision rule → Claim`.

A missing link is an evidence gap, not an invitation to fill the story with prose.

## Evidence proximity

Keep evidential relevance separate from source prestige.

- `A` — direct evidence from the target project/system with an appropriate design;
- `B` — direct evidence from a highly similar system;
- `C` — adjacent-system evidence supporting plausibility;
- `D` — inference from established principles;
- `E` — working hypothesis or speculation.

Source quality is a separate field such as `primary`, `systematic review`, `methods standard`, `preprint`, or `secondary summary`.

## Decisive experiment schema

For every proposed experiment record:

- target claim;
- competing hypotheses;
- independent experimental unit;
- treatment/intervention;
- strongest baseline;
- nuisance variables and blocking/randomization needs;
- required controls;
- direct measurements;
- proxy measurements and their limitations;
- expected pattern under each hypothesis;
- falsifying result;
- statistical or decision analysis;
- sample, instrument, cost, time, safety, and feasibility constraints;
- what decision changes if the result is positive, negative, or indeterminate.

## Controls

Use controls because they block a named alternative explanation.

Possible roles:

- untreated or concurrent baseline;
- vehicle/procedural control;
- positive control;
- negative control;
- mechanism-specific perturbation;
- pH-, ionic-strength-, mass-, energy-, or composition-matched control;
- batch, position, day, operator, or instrument block.

Do not add a control whose explanatory role cannot be stated.

## Experiment priority

The conceptual objective is to maximize useful learning per unit resource.

Consider:

- expected information gain;
- ability to discriminate named hypotheses;
- decision relevance;
- irreversibility of the decision;
- cost and time;
- experimental feasibility;
- downstream dependency: whether later experiments become interpretable only after this one.

Use qualitative `P0/P1/P2` or ordinal ranks unless probabilities and utilities are genuinely calibrated.

### P0

A cheap or necessary experiment that can kill the main idea, separate the strongest rivals, validate the measurement, or determine whether expensive downstream work is interpretable.

### P1

An experiment that strengthens the mechanism, estimates robustness, or closes an important but non-blocking evidence gap.

### P2

A completeness, generalization, sensitivity, or publication-polish experiment that should not precede unresolved P0 questions.

## Evidence architecture and figures

The master evidence architecture may later become a main figure, but the research logic must determine the figure rather than the reverse.

If new data reject an early mechanism, revise the framework. Do not preserve an attractive figure at the cost of scientific truth.
